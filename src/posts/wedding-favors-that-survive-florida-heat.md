Let's talk about the small, sad graveyard at the back of every Florida wedding reception.

You know the one. The table by the exit, lined with little organza bags or monogrammed boxes, lovingly assembled at 11pm three Tuesdays ago by you, your mom, and a bottle of sauvignon blanc. By the end of the night, two-thirds of those favors are still sitting there, judging the guests who sprinted past them toward the valet. By Monday, housekeeping is sweeping them into a black trash bag.

It is, objectively, one of the most heartbreaking moments in wedding planning. Not because the favors were bad. Because they were chocolate. In August. In Florida.

So before you spend $4 a head on something destined for the dumpster, let's have the favor talk. Friend to friend. Over a metaphorical glass of wine.

## The Chocolate Massacre of Every Florida Summer

Here's what nobody tells you when Pinterest shows you those gorgeous little boxes of Belgian truffles tied with silk ribbon: chocolate has a melting point, and Florida has opinions about it.

I watched a bride in June 2024 hand out custom chocolate bars at a stunning courtyard ceremony. Twelve minutes between the favor table and the cocktail hour. By the time guests sat down for dinner, the chocolate had bonded with the foil wrapper in a way that felt almost romantic, if you squinted. Several grandmothers got it on their dresses. One ring bearer ate his with the wrapper still attached. The bride cried — not the good kind.

The rules of what wilts, melts, drips, or molds in a Florida summer are non-negotiable, and they are these:

**Chocolate** doesn't survive the walk from the favor table to the Uber. Even "heat-stable" chocolate, which is a phrase invented by people who have never been to Tampa in July. If you absolutely must, hand it out as guests leave, in an air-conditioned vestibule, and accept that 30% will still puddle.

**Candles** are a special kind of betrayal. They look gorgeous in the centerpiece tablescape photos. They turn into soft little Dalí sculptures by dessert. A pillar candle favor at an outdoor reception in Naples last summer slumped sideways in its glass jar before the toasts were over. Pretty? In a surrealist way. Giftable? No.

**Fresh flowers as favors** — those darling "take a stem from the centerpiece" moments — are gorgeous in theory and devastating in practice. Eucalyptus wilts in forty-five minutes in real Florida humidity. Hydrangea heads bow their heads in sympathy. By the time guests get them home, they're potpourri.

**Macarons** are basically French sugar with a humidity allergy. They get tacky, then chewy, then tragic.

**Bath salts in muslin bags**, weirdly, also fail. The salts absorb the air's moisture and turn into one solid brick. Charming if you're going for an art project. Less charming if Aunt Linda was hoping for a spa night.

## What Guests Actually Take Home

Here's the thing I wish someone had told me before my own wedding: the best favor is one that solves a real problem your guest is having *that night*. Not a future problem. Not a sentimental problem. A right-now-at-this-wedding problem.

Florida summer guests have very specific problems. Their feet hurt. They're sweating through their hair. The mosquitoes have located them. The sun is doing something violent to their shoulders during the cocktail hour. Their tequila is warm.

Favors that solve those problems? Get used. Get loved. Get talked about.

A basket of cheap white flip-flops by the dance floor — the kind you can buy in bulk for under $2 a pair — is the single most-used favor I have ever seen at a Florida wedding. Every. Single. One. gets taken. The bride who did this at a beachfront reception at the **[Anna Maria Island Inn](/venues/anna-maria-island-inn-bradenton-beach)** — that intimate Bradenton Beach property that hosts maybe 80 guests at most and feels more like a friend's gorgeous beach house than a venue — said it was the best $140 she spent on the whole wedding.

A hand fan printed with the ceremony program — old church-wedding trick, brilliantly recycled — is everything when you're sitting in white plastic chairs in a sunny garden ceremony. I've seen this work beautifully at the **[Audubon House & Tropical Gardens](/venues/audubon-house-and-tropical-gardens-key-west)** in Key West, where the gardens are stunning and the breeze is theoretical. Guests fanned themselves through the vows and took the fans home as accidental keepsakes.

Mini bottles of sunscreen at outdoor ceremonies. Bug spray wipes in pretty little envelopes — yes, really, and yes, your guests will love you. Sunglasses tied to the welcome table for an afternoon ceremony in the **[Bonnet House Museum & Gardens](/venues/bonnet-house-museum-and-gardens-fort-lauderdale)** in Fort Lauderdale, where the light bounces off the white walls in a way that's romantic for photos and brutal for unprotected pupils.

These feel like favors. They function like survival kits. They're brilliant.

## The Edible Loophole

If you're set on a food favor — and many are, because it feels generous and personal — there are exactly three categories that survive Florida.

Local honey in small jars, sourced from a farmer's market or a Florida apiary, is gorgeous and indestructible. Customize the label, cap it with kraft paper and twine, done. A jar of orange blossom honey at a wedding I attended at **[Bella Vista Ranch](/venues/bella-vista-ranch-naples)** — that gorgeous Naples garden property — felt so right it was almost on the nose. Most guests had never had real Florida honey before. Several texted the bride about it weeks later.

Hot sauce, which Florida couples have been giving away under the pun "hot sauce for our hot love" for at least a decade now and somehow it still works. Small batch, pretty bottle, custom label. It survives a glove compartment, a checked bag, and a divorce.

And cookies — but specifically *shortbread* or *biscotti*, the dry, structurally-sound cookies. Not anything iced. Not anything filled. Royal icing in Florida humidity is a slow-motion crime scene. A Cuban couple I know did guava-and-cheese pastelitos individually wrapped at a reception at **[Hialeah Park Racing & Casino](/venues/hialeah-park-racing-and-casino-hialeah)**, that gorgeous historic landmark, and the favors were hand-delivered to each table at the very end of the night, not displayed for hours. Smart. Specific. Personal. Eaten in the car on the way home.

## What "Personal" Actually Means

Here's where I want to push back on something: the wedding industry has convinced couples that "personal" means "monogrammed." That if you put your initials and the date on a thing, it counts as meaningful.

It does not. It counts as inventory.

A truly personal favor is one that says something specific about *you*, the couple. Not your monogram. Your taste. Your story. Your inside joke.

The couple who got engaged on a sailing trip and gave away tiny bottles of the local rum from the island where it happened — that's personal. The bride who's a ceramicist and made 90 tiny hand-thrown dishes for her wedding at the **[Ancient Spanish Monastery](/venues/ancient-spanish-monastery-north-miami-beach)** in North Miami Beach (capacity around 200, and yes, she made all 90 herself over four months and had a small breakdown around piece 60) — those favors are now on bedside tables across three time zones. The couple who met at a coffee shop and gifted small bags of beans from that exact roaster — that's a story you can drink.

None of those favors required a melting point chart. All of them got taken home.

## A Practical Word About Money

Let's talk numbers, since this is where I actually earn my keep.

Favors in 2026 are running roughly $3-8 a head if you're DIY-ing thoughtfully, $8-15 a head for elevated stuff (small-batch local goods, hand-poured anything, decent ceramics), and into the $20+ range if you're doing something genuinely custom or experiential. For a 120-guest wedding, that's a budget line of anywhere from $360 to $2,400+, which is not nothing.

My honest, unromantic recommendation: cap it at $5 a head and put it toward something every guest will actually use that night. The flip-flop basket. The fan-program. The water bottles in a galvanized tub at the ceremony entrance. The printed cocktail napkins with the signature drink recipe (these get pocketed constantly, by the way — I have seen grown men fold them into their wallets).

If you have a bigger budget and you really want to do the elevated edible favor, *hand it out at the end of the night*. Have the catering staff distribute them like a goodbye gift as guests head to the exit. This is the single biggest fix for the favor-graveyard problem and almost no one does it. A reception coordinator at **[520 on the Water](/venues/520-on-the-water-sanford)** in Sanford told me they've started suggesting this to every couple, and the rate of favors-actually-taken-home went from about 40% to nearly 100%. Same favor. Different timing. Total transformation.

## The Florida-Specific Favor Hall of Fame

A few that have stayed with me across hundreds of weddings:

A welcome bag of citrus and a handwritten note at a destination wedding at **[Baker's Cay Resort Key Largo](/venues/bakers-cay-resort-key-largo)** — not the favor at the reception, but the welcome favor at the hotel room. Guests cried. The bride did not break the bank. Total cost per bag, around $11.

A late-night Cuban coffee bar at **[Bagatelle Restaurant](/venues/bagatelle-restaurant-key-west)** in Key West, where guests took home a tiny canister of espresso grounds and the recipe for a cortadito. Functional. Personal. Specifically *Florida*.

A s'mores kit at a beachfront reception, with the marshmallows, chocolate, and graham crackers individually wrapped — yes, with chocolate, but distributed at the *end* of the night near the bonfire, where guests roasted them on the spot and didn't take home a single melted thing. The favor *was* the moment.

The pattern, if you're looking for one: the best Florida wedding favors are the ones the couple thought about as a *gift* and not as an obligation. They solved a problem, told a story, or created a moment. They were not sitting in a sad pile by the exit at 11pm.

## The Real Secret

Here is what I want you to take away from all of this, more than any specific recommendation:

The favor is the smallest line item on your wedding budget that says the most about how you think. About whether you're choosing things because they're trendy or because they're true. About whether you're planning a wedding to impress people or to take care of them.

A good favor in Florida is just a small act of love that doesn't melt.

Which, when I write it out like that, sounds like the whole point of the wedding in the first place.
